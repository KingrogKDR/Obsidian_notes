The formula was called, in the ledgers, Pinia-8. A working name, meant to be replaced once it did what Dr. Ilan Vosk built it to do: end death from disease, permanently, for everyone.

On his desk sat a photograph of a girl of nine, his own figure leaning over her and hugging her tight, both smiling. She had died of a fever four doctors had called by four different names. He kept the photograph there like other men kept scripture. As a reminder of why he was doing this.

He hadn't slept properly in three years. His notebooks said so, in handwriting that got worse every month.

The compound had stopped acting like medicine weeks earlier, though he hadn't told the board that. A wound would close, and keep closing, growing new skin over new skin until someone had to stop a technician from cutting it back open just to check. A fever would break and never come back, but the patient's temperature never quite returned to normal either. In trials that never made it into any official record, the test mice stopped aging in ways his instruments could measure but he couldn't explain.

Tonight was supposed to fix that. A stabilizing dose. He gave it to himself, because he'd run out of mice he trusted and years he was willing to spend waiting for permission.

He remembers the needle going in clean. He remembers the cold of it reaching his elbow faster than it should have. He does not remember deciding to scream.

He remembers looking at his own hand and seeing the fingers had grown a half-inch longer than they'd been a second before. The nail beds had gone black, not bruised, just black, like ink under glass. Then the light in the room changed, before the pain even caught up to him.

---

Three technicians were in the lab that night. None of them agree, afterward, on how long it lasted. All three agree Vosk was still standing when it stopped. Someone wrote that down later as _the first irregularity_ as if a man surviving what had just happened to his own hand were simply a clerical detail.

One of them, a woman named Mira Oss, lived a lot longer than anyone expected her to. Decades later, worn down by a niece who wouldn't let it go, she said this much and no more: it hadn't felt like something coming into the room. It had felt like the room finally admitting it had never been empty. And whatever had been waiting in that emptiness walked out through the nearest door it could find.

The nearest door was Ilan Vosk.

She never looked at him properly again. She didn't need to. In the second before she looked away, she saw his eyes had gone the same black as his fingernails and that he was smiling, like a man who'd finally gotten an answer to a question he longed to be to be solved.

---

By morning, the compound was gone from every container that had held it. Some say it was a failure. Others say it was stolen. Just finished, like it had stopped being a liquid the same hour it stopped being medicine. Vosk lived. His hands didn't stay his own. They changed and for the worse.

He survived eleven more years. Long enough to watch the word _mutation_ replace the word _miracle_ in every report but his own. Long enough to see others start changing near him — and then, more and more. In the last readable line of his last notebook, kept out of public record for almost a century, he wrote only this:

_I did not open it for them. I opened it, and it chose, afterward, to be generous._

He never explained what he meant by _it_. He kept the photograph until the end. He never figured out — not even for himself — whether he'd finally built the cure his daughter needed, nine years too late. Or whether something else had simply been waiting, all that time, for a day like the Catastrophe.
