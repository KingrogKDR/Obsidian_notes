The streak was the first thing people noticed and the last thing they mentioned, which was, Ryger had decided some years ago, the most Orman way a thing could be handled.

It ran from his left temple back through the hair above his ear — a finger's width of pale, colorless white against black. Not grey. Nothing so ordinary for his age. Just a patch of him that had finished growing right. His mother kept that side of his hair trimmed shorter than the other, ever since he was small. She said it was only to keep it tidy. He never believed her, and never said so.

He was combing water through it now in the cold morning light off the practice yard, watching the streak go dark and pale as the light shifted, when Serel found him.

"You're wanted," she said from the doorway, in the voice she always used for him. Disapproval. Serel had served his mother longer than Ryger had been alive. It was most of what she was made of. "Steward's called the yard for first bells. The lord wants you. Specifically."

"Specifically."

"That's the word he used." She said it again like it tasted bad. "So stop admiring yourself in that basin."

"I wasn't admiring myself."

"You were looking, which at your age is close enough." She was already turning to go — that was how Serel ended a conversation, whether or not you were finished with it. "First bells. Don't make him ask twice. Not this week."

_Not this week._ She said things like that often, small pieces of something larger she never finished handing over. She was gone before he could ask what she meant, her footsteps already fading toward the kitchens.

He finished with his hair badly, same as always, and went to find out what his uncle wanted badly enough to call for him by name.

---

The practice yard at first light belonged to the house guard by right and to whoever got there first in practice. This morning that was his cousin — down at the far end, a wooden blade in each hand, working through a drill their arms-master had given him weeks ago and clearly not forgiven him for since.

Adan was eleven. He fought like a boy who'd been told, correctly, that he'd be a lord one day, and had somehow also concluded, incorrectly, that this meant the world owed him patience while he learned how.

"You're late," Adan called, not stopping.

"I'm not late. I'm _sent for_." Ryger leaned against the yard fence, arms crossed. "Drop your right shoulder. You lead with it every third strike, and Ferro's going to make you feel stupid about it the second he's awake."

Adan dropped his shoulder. Kept going.

"Uncle wants me. Specifically," Ryger said, mostly to see what his cousin would do with it.

That got him to stop. "Specifically," Adan repeated, the same way Serel had, and for a second something moved across his face that wasn't quite jealousy — Adan wasn't old enough yet to hide behind jealousy properly — something more like the plain discomfort of a boy who already understood, without ever being told outright, that his own future bent a little around the older cousin standing at the fence. Nobody had explained the succession to Adan. Nobody had needed to. Houses like Ashvael raised children who understood their own inheritance before they could spell their names.

"Might be nothing," Ryger said. It was a kindness. It was also a lie. They both knew it.

Adan went back to his drill. Dropped his shoulder again on his own, without being told this time. Ryger watched him a moment longer — there was something good in watching a boy get better at a thing in real time, uncomplicated in a way little else in this house was — then went to find out what his uncle wanted, before he was late twice in one morning.

---

His uncle's study smelled the way it always did: lamp oil, and the dry, papery smell of letters that had been read too many times. Lord Ashvael was standing when Ryger came in, not sitting, and that alone was worth noting. His uncle sat for ordinary business. He stood for things that hadn't yet decided how they wanted to be said.

"Shut the door," said Tigor Ashvael. Then,  gentler, once it was shut: "Sit. This isn't a scolding."

"Serel made it sound like one."

"Serel makes everything sound like one. It's how she shows she cares." A small smile, there and gone. His uncle didn't smile easily — not for lack of kindness, everyone said Lord Ashvael had more of that than his position required, but because he'd learned, the way men who held a border seat learned, that a smile in the wrong room could be mistaken for a promise.

He didn't sit either, in the end. He went to the map table instead — the one with the long scar of the Godscar inked across it, dozens of small pins marking things Ryger had never been told the meaning of and had stopped asking about, because the answer was always some version of _not yet, when you're older_.

"A rider came up from the Ferry Road stations. Two nights back." His uncle spoke to the map, not to him. "Nothing urgent enough to wake the house for. Which is strange, given who it was about."

"Who was it about?"

His uncle looked up. There was something in his face Ryger didn't have a name for yet — maybe worry, maybe the careful blankness he wore for house business. Something underneath both.

"You," he said. "Or close enough to you that I wanted you to hear it from me. Not from Serel's version of it by supper."

Ryger waited. His uncle arrived at things in his own time, and pushing him only slowed him down — a lesson that had taken years, and more patience than Ryger naturally had, to learn.

"House Corvain sent a formal offer. For you." His uncle turned from the map at last. "Their daughter. Lae, the second one, not the eldest. She comes of age this spring. They want the betrothal settled before then."

Whatever Ryger had thought in his head between the fence and this room — this wasn't it. He expected it to be something urgent. Something that involved him taking a ride to the borderlands and finally be killed. He was weak, he knew that. Everyone knew that.  

Hearing those words filled him with relief, before anything else had the chance to. He was almost ashamed of how much of it there was.

"Corvain," he said, mostly to have something to say. "They've never sent us so much as a letter at midwinter."

"No. Which is exactly my concern — and why I called you here first, ahead of council." He looked at Ryger properly now, not with the Lord-of-Ashvael face but the one that belonged to a man who had simply loved his nephew and asked nothing back for it. "Corvain doesn't send offers, Ryger. Corvain receives them. For them to come to us first — to come to _you_ first, before any word reached me as your guardian, before any of the usual courtesies — that isn't a marriage offer. That's a house testing whether we'll bend our neck to be asked. I want you to understand what it means, one way or another, before anyone outside this room decides for you how you're meant to feel about it."

The pins on the map caught the light as he moved past them again. Small. Unreadable on their own. Ryger looked at them and thought, for the first time with any real clarity, that they weren't so different from him — small fixed points that meant nothing alone, and everything, once someone decided how to read the shape they made together.

---

He didn't go back to the yard. He told himself it was because Adan didn't need an audience for the rest of his drill, and that was even partly true.

Mostly he wanted his mother.

He found her in the still-room, sleeves pinned back, elbow-deep in the business of pressing oil from something that smelled sharp enough to sting his eyes from the doorway. She didn't look up. She never had to. She always knew it was him before he said anything, the way she always seemed to know most things about him before he worked out how to say them himself.

"Your uncle told you, then."

"You knew?"

"I knew there was a rider. I didn't know what he carried until an hour ago." She set the press down and wiped her hands on the cloth at her hip, and only then did she look at him properly. "Corvain."

"He said it might not really be about me."

"Your uncle is careful with what he tells you. That doesn't mean it's the whole of it." She said it gently, but she said it, which was more than most people in this house bothered to. "How do you feel?"

It was the kind of question that deserved an honest answer and he gave her one anyway, because she was the one person he never quite managed to lie to, even when lying would have cost him nothing.

"Relieved." He said it low, like it embarrassed him, because it did. "I thought it was going to be something worse. The border. Or — I don't know. Something that needed me to actually be useful for once."

She didn't tell him that wasn't true, that he was useful, that he mattered to this house in ways beyond what he could lift or strike or survive. She'd said those things before, and he'd stopped quite believing them somewhere around the age he'd stopped believing the streak in his hair was only ever about tidiness. Instead she reached out and set two fingers, briefly, against the side of his face — the way she'd done since he was small enough to be carried on a hip instead of standing here nearly as tall as she was.

"Then be relieved," she said. "There will be enough days that ask more of you than you have to give. Let this not be one of them, if it doesn't have to be."

He wanted to believe that was the end of it. Some part of him — the part that loved her without complication or condition, the part that would have said yes to nearly anything she asked of him — wanted very badly to believe her.

He was fairly sure, even as he nodded, that neither of them fully did.

---

They lit the candles at dusk, the way they did every year on this day.

There were thirteen of them set along the long table in the hall — one for each year since Timir Ashvael had been killed by Truman hands on the far side of the Godscar, in a skirmish small enough that most of the continent had already forgotten it happened at all. House Ashvael had not forgotten. House Ashvael, Ryger had come to understand, was not a house built to forget things.

Tigor lit the first candle himself, as he did every year, and said the same handful of words he always said — plain ones, unpolished, more like a man talking to his brother across a room than reciting anything formal. Ryger had heard the words enough times that he could have said them himself, and had never once heard Tigor say them without his voice catching, just slightly, on the same word every time.

Risc stood behind Yuna's chair through all of it, one hand resting on her shoulder, saying nothing. He rarely said much on this night. He was a big man, quiet and still, and Ryger had grown up thinking of that stillness as simply what a father was — solid, unhurried, present without needing to fill the room to prove it. It was only in the last year or two that he'd begun to notice how careful Risc was with his own hands, always, as though some old habit of restraint had never quite worn off him. Ryger didn't know why. He had never thought to ask, the way children rarely think to ask about the things that have simply always been true.

Adan sat across from him, unusually still for a boy. He hadn't known Timir. He'd been born after. But grief in a house like this one didn't require memory to be inherited — only proximity, and enough years of watching the people who did remember.

When it was Ryger's turn, he said what he always said, which was not much. He wasn't sure, whether he was mourning a man he had never met or simply mourning the shape of an absence he'd been handed at birth and told to carry. Tonight, watching his mother's face in the candlelight — steady, composed, and, underneath that, maybe guilt, sadness — he found himself mourning that too, whatever it was. 

The candles burned low. Nobody spoke of Corvain, or borders, or anything else. For one night, House Ashvael allowed itself to be only a family, gathered around a loss thirteen years old and still, somehow, not finished healing.

Ryger went to bed that night thinking of Lae Corvain's name, and felt guilty for it, and thought of it anyway.

---

Tigor read the letter a third time. He still could not find it forgivable.

House Corvain wished to propose a union between their daughter, Lae, and Ryger Vay Ashvael, heir to House Ashvael's border stewardship — the letter said, addressed, sealed, and delivered directly to the boy himself, bypassing Tigor's seat entirely. Not through the temple registrar. Not through a proxy. Such was their way of showing courtesy to the Lord and the House itself.

"They knew exactly what they were doing," Tigor said, and set the letter on the table between them, face down.

Yuna sat across from him with her hands folded in her lap, still and composed in the way when she was working hard to look calmer than she was. Risc stood by the window with his arms crossed, saying nothing yet, which was usually how Risc started a conversation.

"And they don't care," he said. "Which might be worse."

"A branch house tests its luck skipping channels," Tigor said. "Corvain doesn't need to test anything. They're one of the Eight. Gemma's own house, older than half the pantheon's temples. They don't send a letter like this by accident, and they don't send it out of ignorance of the insult." He rubbed at his eyes. Thirteen years running this house and he still hadn't found the trick of making the ache behind them go away before council. "This is a hand on the table. They want to see if we'll flinch."

"Or if we'll bend. There's a difference."

"Is there," Tigor said, not really a question.

Risc turned from the glass. He had a way of standing that made the room feel smaller without him ever raising his voice — old muscle memory, Tigor guessed, from a life his brother-in-law had walked away from and never once spoken of directly. "If you send it back unanswered, that's a refusal, and refusing one of the Eight over a breach of etiquette costs you more than the etiquette was worth. If you answer it as written, you've let them set the terms — a Great House talking to a fourteen-year-old boy like he's already the one holding the seat. Either way, you've told them something about how far Ashvael bends."

"So we don't do either," Yuna said.

Tigor looked at her. She rarely spoke first in these councils, because she preferred, he'd noticed over the years, to let a problem finish revealing itself before she touched it. It was one of the things that had made her formidable as Lady Ashvael, the Valkyrie of Ashvael, and made her, now, formidable as something else entirely — a mother perhaps.

"There's a third door," she went on. "Return it through the temple registrar, properly, as though the breach never happened — as though we're simply correcting a clerical oversight on their part, not calling it what it is. Let them decide whether they want to be embarrassed enough to pursue it the right way, or whether the whole gesture quietly dies of politeness." A small, humorless smile. "Petty, corrects nothing, costs them nothing they'll admit to losing. But it puts the next move back in their hands, on our terms."

Tigor found that he mostly agreed, and disliked that he mostly agreed, because it meant doing nothing. But his instincts wanted him to do something. 

"You don't trust it's really about the boy," he said to her, not quite a question either.

"I don't trust anything from that house is really about the thing it says it's about," Yuna said. "Corvain doesn't need our border strength for a wedding. If they want it, they want it for something a wedding gives them access to."

Risc didn't disagree. 

"Adan's included in this, whether we say his name or not," Tigor said, mostly to himself. He thought of his son in the yard that morning, running the same drill Ryger had needled him about, red-faced and diligent and twelve years old and entirely unaware that a letter existed. Tigor loved his son without complication. He loved his nephew the same way, which was the harder thing, some days, to hold both at once. "He'll ask why the offer went to Ryger and not him."

"He already knows why," Risc said, not unkindly.

"Knowing and asking aren't the same thing," Tigor said, and reached for the letter again, turning it face up. Then he called for Serel, who arrived quickly enough that Tigor suspected she'd been standing just past the door the whole time — a suspicion he'd long since stopped voicing, since Serel always denied it with a face too innocent to be true and he'd never once caught her at it.

"Have this copied for the registrar," he told her. "Word for word, no note attached. And find out — quietly — whether Corvain's had any other correspondence out this season. To anyone."

Serel took the letter without comment.

When she'd gone, Yuna finally let herself look tired. "You didn't tell Ryger any of this."

"I told him it was a formal offer and that I'd handle it," Tigor said. "Which is true."

"It's not the whole truth."

"He's fourteen," Tigor said. "He'll have the whole truth soon enough."

Yuna seemed dissatisfied, and Risc's hand found her shoulder without either of them seeming to notice it happen, and Tigor looked back down at the space on the table where the letter had been, already half-composing, in his head, the careful nothing he was about to send back to one of the eight most dangerous families on the continent.

---

Ambrose Corvain heard about the redirected letter four days later, over breakfast, from a man whose entire function in the household was to know things before Ambrose asked.

"Sent back through the registrar," Coen said, setting the report beside the fruit. "No refusal. No acceptance. Not even a note. Filed as a clerical correction, as if seemingly it was our fault for sending it to the wrong person."

Ambrose almost smiled. He didn't, because Lae was at the table, and he'd long ago decided that whatever games were being played with her future, she would not watch him enjoy playing them.

"Clever," he said instead, mildly, and reached for his cup. "Tell me Tigor Ashvael came up with that himself and not his sister."

"Does it matter which of them it was?"

"It tells me which one to watch." Ambrose turned the cup slowly on its saucer, a habit Coen had learned to read of his lord. "Tigor is a soldier who inherited a house. He thinks in terms of ground held and ground given. His sister thinks in terms of doors — which ones to open, which to leave shut, which to leave _almost_ shut so she can watch who tries them." He set the cup down. "That was her answer. It buys them a season, maybe two, before anyone's obligated to say yes or no to anything. And it tells us, without telling us anything at all, that House Ashvael intends to be difficult about this in a way that costs them nothing."

"Difficult isn't the same as unwilling," Coen said.

"No," Ambrose agreed. "It rarely is, with a house that has more pride than coin."

Lae had not touched her breakfast. She was fifteen, composed in the way her mother had trained into her since she was old enough to sit still at a table like this one, and Ambrose knew — because he made it his business to know everything under his own roof twice as well as he knew things outside it — that she had read the letter herself before it ever left the house. He had not stopped her. There was very little point pretending this concerned her only in the abstract.

"You could simply ask me what I think of him," she said, without looking up.

"I could," Ambrose said. "Do you think anything of him yet? You've never met the boy."

"I think a house that sends its heir's marriage offer as a message to a rival family instead of a proposal to a person isn't very interested in what I think of him."

Coen, wisely, said nothing. Ambrose set his cup down fully this time and gave his daughter the attention she'd earned by saying something true and inconvenient at his own table.

"You're not wrong," he said. "But you're not entirely right, either. It can be both. A message, and, eventually, if the pieces fall the way I expect them to, a marriage that suits you better than most of the ones I could arrange. Ashvael raises decent men. I've made worse bets on better houses." He didn't add the rest of it — that decent men made useful husbands precisely because they were slower to suspect what was really being asked of them, or of the house they married into, or of the boy standing quietly at its center with no idea yet what he was for. He had not decided, even in his own mind, exactly how much of what came next ran through Ryger Ashvael and how much ran _past_ him. It didn't need deciding yet. Ashvael had just bought everyone a season. Ambrose intended to spend every day of it.

"Send our reply through the registrar as well," he told Coen, once Lae had excused herself. "Slower than theirs. Let them wonder if we're insulted."

"Are we?"

Ambrose looked out at the grounds — the fountains that ran even in the driest months, the coin-and-flame sigil of Gemma worked into every gate on the estate, wealth as visible and unapologetic as Ashvael's martial iron was plain and unadorned. Two houses that had never needed to raise their voices to be heard.

"We're patient," he said. "It reads the same from a distance, and it costs considerably less."

---

Yuna found Ryger in the still-room again, which was becoming, he thought, something of a pattern between them — as though whenever there was news he'd rather not hear alone, his feet carried him to his mother before his mind had decided to go.

"It's been sent back," she told him, without making him ask. "To the temple registrar, the way it should have come to begin with. Your uncle's not refusing it. He's correcting it. If House Corvain wants to pursue this properly, they'll have the chance to."

"And if they don't?"

"Then it was never really about you in the first place, and we've lost nothing by finding that out slowly instead of quickly." She said it gently, the way she said most hard things to him. He didn't ask what she wasn't saying. He'd learned that, without ever deciding to — that there were rooms in his mother's thoughts he wasn't invited into, and that pushing at the door only ever made her go quiet in a way that frightened him more than any answer could have.

He thought, guiltily, that he was relieved again. A season, maybe more, before anything had to be real. He turned that relief over and found, underneath it, something that surprised him: a small, unwelcome disappointment, there and gone before he could examine it honestly, that it hadn't simply been decided and finished.

He didn't realize that a girl he had never met already existed in his imagination. He couldn't picture her face, only a vague shape of her existing somewhere in his future. He filed it away with the rest of the things he didn't understand about himself and went to find Adan, because standing still with his own thoughts for too long had never once gone well for him.

Adan was in the yard, working through the same drill from two days before, and this time he didn't stop when Ryger arrived.

"Dad told me," Adan said, not looking up from his footwork. "About the letter."

"I figured he would."

"He didn't tell me why it went to you and not through him first. He just said it had been handled." Adan's blade came around a half-second late on the turn, the same fault as before, and this time Ryger didn't correct it. "I know why, before you say it. I know I'm not the heir. You don't have to explain the succession to me, Ryger, I was there when I was born into it."

"I wasn't going to explain anything," Ryger said, and meant it, though he heard, underneath Adan's flat tone, something that wanted an apology he didn't know how to give without making it worse. They stood there a moment, and Ryger felt the old, familiar shape of the thing between them — something others would call rivalry, but what he would call awkwardness.

"Lae Corvain," Adan said finally. "Is she pretty?"

"I don't know. I've never seen her."

"You're thinking about her anyway."

Ryger didn't answer that, which was, itself, an answer, and Adan — younger, sharper than anyone gave him credit for — let it go and reset his stance for another pass.

Above the practice yard, past the wall, the land stretched toward the Godscar the way it always had, quiet and unremarkable in the afternoon light, and Ryger didn't look at it, because there was nothing to look at. Yet.

He went back to drilling with Adan until the light went gold and then grey, and thought, before he let himself sleep that night, of a name and no face, and of a season that had just quietly begun.
