The ward-stone at Post Six had a chip in its northern face shaped, if you squinted and had spent eleven years staring at it, like a running dog. Davin Kest had spent eleven years staring at it. He knew the dog. He'd named it, privately, Biscuit, on a night watch so cold he'd needed something stupid to think about or go mad, and he still checked on it every morning the way another man might check that his own front door was still standing.

This morning the dog was fine. The rest of the post was not.

"You're doing the thing," said Pell, from the brazier, where he was failing, as usual, to get the kindling to take on the first try. "The face. Where you look at the stone like it insulted your mother."

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing a face."

Davin crouched by the ward-stone anyway and laid two fingers against Biscuit's chipped ear, and did the thing he'd done every morning of his service, the thing that had stopped feeling like a small miracle sometime around his third year and gone back to feeling like one again sometime around his eighth, for reasons he'd never fully worked out. He spoke the old words — not loud, they didn't need to be loud, the Eight weren't hard of hearing, whatever the new recruits thought — and felt the stone answer the way it always answered: a low give, like a held breath finally let out, and then the pull.

Cold bloomed across the backs of his hands almost at once, fast enough to sting, the way it always did. That was the whole of it, really, however the temple liked to dress it up in ceremony and old language — you asked the stone to hold its shape against the frost and rot the border threw at it day and night, and something had to carry what the stone gave up in order to keep holding. Tonight it was him. His hands would ache with a cold that had nothing to do with the weather for the rest of the shift, and tomorrow they'd ache a little less, and in thirty years, if the temple physicians were honest with him, which they mostly weren't, his hands would ache all the time and not stop. That was the bargain. Nobody who took the oath was lied to about the bargain.

Biscuit's ear held steady under his fingers. Warded. Whole.

"Section Nine's not answering the relay," Pell said, and this time there was no humor in it at all, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted.

Davin was on his feet before he'd finished deciding to be.

---

The walk to Section Nine took the better part of an hour along the ward-line, and Davin spent most of it doing the thing every border-post soldier did without meaning to: counting stones. Not literally. Counting the rhythm of them, the small unconscious beat of reassurance that kept a man sane after eleven years on the border. Godscar wasn't a wall the way people pictured it from the interior, all clean stone and neat crenellations. It was a long, patched, homely scar of a thing — old battlements grown over with grass in places, watch-towers standing a mile apart with nothing but warded stakes and prayer between them, smugglers' paths worn into the dirt on both sides of it that everyone knew about and nobody with any sense reported. It didn't need to be a wall. It needed to hold.

Section Nine's ward-post was cracked down the middle.

Davin had seen ward-stones fail before — twice in eleven years, both times slow, both times the kind of failure you could see coming if you knew where to look at. This wasn't that. This was a stone split clean, and one half of the crack was scorched black, the surface bubbled like it had spent an hour in a forge, and the other half of the exact same crack was rimed white with frost so thick it hadn't melted despite the sun being well up. Steam curled off the black half. Frost still crept, visibly, crack by crack, across the white — growing, right in front of him, in full daylight.

"That's not possible," Pell said, which was the correct thing to say and also useless.

"No," Davin agreed. He crouched by the stone the way he had at Post Six, and made himself put his fingers near the crack. He didn't touch it. Heat came off the black side in a thin, dry wave, the kind that dried the inside of your nose. Off the white side, a hand's width away, came cold so sharp it made his back teeth ache. Both at once, off the same stone, an inch apart. His stomach rolled. He swallowed twice and made himself keep looking instead of turning away.

He knew the shape of a heat-working. He knew the shape of a frost-working. He'd done both a thousand times, felt both a thousand times, could have described either to a child. What he was looking at wasn't either one laid over the other. It was both of them run through the same channel at the same instant, feeding each other instead of fighting, and the only reason his mind had a name ready for it was a lecture from his second year of temple training he hadn't thought about in a decade.

His instructor had stood in front of the novice hall with two candles, one in each hand, flames lit, and told them to watch. She'd brought the flames together, wick to wick, slowly, until they touched — and both had guttered out at once, smothered by their own closeness, leaving her standing in the dark with two threads of smoke rising. _That's what happens when you're careless,_ she'd said. _Now imagine wanting it to happen. Imagine reaching for it on purpose._ Nobody in that room had understood, at fourteen, why her voice had shaken saying it. Davin understood now, crouched over a stone doing exactly what she'd shown them, deliberately.

Braiding.

Two rites weren't meant to share a hand. Every rite you learned, you learned alone, clean and pure, its cost landing in one place you'd agreed to carry — that was the discipline the whole oath rested on: _one thing at a time_, *one clean debt*, *honestly paid* . To run two together wasn't just dangerous. It was a corruption of the whole shape of the bargain, the kind of thing the temple didn't just forbid but actively worked to make its novices unable to _imagine_ wanting. It was blasphemy.

Someone had braided a heat-rite and a frost-rite together, on Orman soil, on an Orman ward-stone, and walked away.

"Where's the relay-keeper," Davin said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the one useful thing eleven years bought a man.

Pell didn't answer immediately, instead hinted at the debris nearby — the boy who should have been standing this post, seventeen years old, temple-marked, half-buried under a collapsed section of the watch-tower's lower wall with an arrow through him that had nothing to do with fire or frost at all.

---

The one who'd done it was still close. Davin understood that, his instincts and experience said so. Pell felt it too. His hand had slowly drifted to his sword hilt.

The figure came out of the treeline unhurried. Orman robes, or what had been Orman robes once, the temple grey gone dull and travel-stained, and — Davin's stomach dropped clean through the floor of him — a rite-mark tattooed along the left wrist, the exact mark every ward-sworn soldier on the Godscar carried, because there was only one place on the continent that gave it to you and only one oath that earned it.

"You're temple," Davin said. It came out stupid, the only thing his mouth could manage.

"I was." No hesitation in it. No shame either, which was worse. "You're going to want to step back from the stone, Kest. It's not stable, and I don't especially want to explain a second body to the people I answer to now."

He knew Davin's name. Davin didn't know him.

"Who," Davin said, "are you answering to."

"People with better questions than the temple ever asked me." The man raised one hand, almost lazily, and Davin felt it before he saw anything. He got a hand on Pell's collar and hauled him sideways off his feet a half-second before the ruined section of watch-tower behind them didn't fall so much as _heave_, stone swelling outward, frost flashing white across the blocks and fire punching through the cracks in the same breath, until the whole wall came apart in a spray of rock, ice, and flame that peppered the ground ten feet from where they'd been standing.

Pell went down hard, alive, cursing.

Then the man was already lifting his hand again, and Davin had no clean rite left in him fast enough to answer with. Eleven years of _one thing at a time, one clean debt, honestly paid_ collapsed into the single ugliest, clearest thought of his life: _the stone doesn't care what you were taught. It only cares what you ask it._

He asked it for both.

Heat tore up his right arm and cold locked around his left in the same instant, and for a moment neither hand would answer him at all. His jaw clenched so hard his teeth sang. His knees buckled. He stayed up anyway.

The ground between himself and the man in temple grey tore open and threw itself upward, black earth and white frost fused into a wall higher than a man's head.

The man staggered one step back and for the first time, surprise crossed his face.

"Well," he said. Interested. "That's new."

He was gone before Davin found the strength to stand, back into the treeline, as if the whole exchange had been a diverting errand rather than the worst four minutes of Davin Kest's life.

---

Pell lived. The relay-boy didn't. Section Nine's ward-post would need rebuilding from the footing up, and the report Davin wrote that night used the word _incursion_ eleven times and the word _braiding_ not once.

He didn't write down what he'd done, either.

He sat on the edge of his cot past midnight with both hands out in front of him under the lamp, turning them over, watching them shake in a way that had nothing to do with cold. The right ached like frostbite. The left ached like a burn. He pressed his palms flat against his knees to make them stop and they didn't stop.

He thought of the two candles again, guttering out together in the dark, and of a stranger with a temple mark on his wrist walking back into the trees.
